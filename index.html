Jaguar7

Turn a brand principle into one manufacturing or craft feat that competitors cannot copy, then use it as the physical proof of the claim.

Why it works

Jaguar's reinvention around the Type 01, a £130,000 electric GT (300bhp, 5.2m long, 0.23 drag) launched in October 2026 from a new Knightsbridge flagship store, with a new roundel, a modernist and art-deco design language, and the principle 'a copy of nothing'.

The brand chose to polarise on purpose: three design teams worked in separate COVID bubbles, and the most radical clay model won 11 of 12 executive votes. A tough product constraint carries the idea. A four-storey, 2,500-ton press cold-forms the 'Impossible Panel' in one pass, from A-pillar to rear bumper across five geometric planes, so 'copy nothing' can be seen and touched. Small details such as the J and r shapes in the headrest stitching and the piano-key motifs on bonnet and dashboard repeat the stance at every touchpoint.

TOSSR6

When a category is stuck on a single claim like strength, invent a harmless victim of your product's superiority and build a familiar cause format (adoption, rescue, petition) around it, so the benefit is implied by the problem it creates.

Why it works

Innocean Australia created a satirical maggot adoption agency for TOSSR's bin bag subscription, where people adopt the maggots left homeless by bags that don't split, with each adoption returning a 20% discount code on a subscription, running on social and owned channels through December 31.

Bin bags are a commoditized category where 'strong' is a claim every brand makes and nobody believes, so the work skips the torture-test demo and invents a victim of the product's strength. The mechanic is a charity-appeal parody (adopt, rehome, save) that gets people to imagine split bags, bin juice and rotting rubbish without being shown them. The product benefit is never stated as a claim, because the maggots' homelessness is the proof, and the discount code gives each adoption a conversion path.

The Whale (Andenes, Norway)7

Make the building's structural logic the metaphor, not an applied graphic, so form and function argue the same point.

Why it works

A whale and ocean knowledge centre in Andenes, Norway, designed by Danish architecture studio Dorte Mandrup with exhibition design by Ralph Appelbaum Associates and Tamschick Media+Space, opening June 2027.

The building itself performs the brand story: a single curved concrete roof surfaces from the rocky shoreline like a whale diving, so the architecture is the metaphor rather than decoration on top of one. It works because the form does double duty functionally too, a column-free exhibition hall below and a public viewpoint on the roof above, so the concept never feels like a gimmick bolted onto a box.
